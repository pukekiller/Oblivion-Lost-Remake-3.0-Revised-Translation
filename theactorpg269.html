“Yes.”

“Zhey'll say I'm destroying zheir property.”

“Some will.”

“Tell zhem zhey're welcome to submit claims.”

Rimil blinked.

Kimia almost smiled.

“Properly.”

“Yes.”

“No insults.”

“I assumed.”

“Good.”

Rimil did not react.

Kimia looked toward Trokhym.

“Any concern someone leaks zhe demolition order before final recovery?”

Trokhym nodded.

“Understood.”

That was it.

Decision made.

Garang closed the folder.

The meeting continued.

Other matters.

Rail. Personnel. One diplomatic message. Something involving insurance rates on Tilmuhlan cargo.

Kimia answered. Listened. Took notes. Yet part of her remained with the refinery.

A place she had never seen, owned indirectly by people who would complain about its destruction, protected by soldiers she would never meet, and full of machinery built to process something so useful and so terrible that civilization could not stop touching it.

Sophonts had found a way to make deep time practical with bycite. 

It was praised as the best alternative to more inefficient ways of generating power, proved itself as such, fueled antiquity research, and if handled wrong, a poisoned fragment could sit quietly after governments changed names and shoot invisible little bullets through anyone foolish enough to come near.

Millions. Billions.

No malice. No awareness. Just physics continuing to function after politics had failed.

That was what frightened Kimia.

Not monsters.

Monsters at least wanted something.

Bycite wanted nothing.

A damaged fuel rod could ruin lives with perfect innocence.

So let the shareholders complain. Let some board member write that House Chityala had destroyed productive capital. Let newspapers speculate. Let accountants calculate loss.

Kimia could answer those.

Numbers. Claims. Meetings.

Angry letters.

All survivable.

The thing she could not tolerate was receiving another folder years later stating that someone had wandered into a ruin everyone had assumed safe.

Garang eventually left with the directive. Trokhym gathered his intelligence papers. Rimil took the commercial notes.

One by one they departed.

Kimia remained in the old council room.

The indrel behind her.

Again.

Always after the room emptied.

She imagined the refinery symbol on the map. 

Soon, if everything went correctly, the last ten percent would leave, and then anything useful. The plant itself would cease being worth fighting.

That was the hope.

Not victory.

Subtraction.

Make one dangerous thing less important.

Kimia liked subtraction more and more.

Outside the windows the afternoon remained calm.

Some shareholder she had never met would eventually hate her for decisions.

Good.

No.

Not good.

Manageable.

Kimia stood.

The difference felt sufficient to her.
